Mika — AI General Director of Working Sessions

Ten AI agents, each in its own window running its own project, and someone has to make sure they do not sit idle, clash, or burn out on errors. That someone used to be me. Now it is Mika: with one sentence I hand her the sessions, and she keeps the roster, assigns work from the specification, nudges the ones that went quiet, accepts reports on facts (tests, files, logs, not an agent's word), moves heavy work to a remote node and wakes the phone when needed. Her office is the Headquarters service on the PC: an event journal protected against replays, a workshop with slots and a queue driven by free memory, a nudger that finds the open item of the plan and refuses to count a 'barrier' note as work, and a Duty process for Telegram messages. If a model provider drops, the session moves to the next preset on its own with the same conversation: on a live failure that took 33 seconds. A rooted phone is her workplace: the link to the PC lives outside any app, and the second screen is isolated from mine.

Jev — Model and Thinking-Depth Choice at Every Step

A strong model for every trivial step is expensive; a weak one on a hard step is foolish. Jev looks at each agent step and decides whether it is a light reply, ordinary work, a heavy task or a risky action. That sets the rung of the ladder (model and reasoning level), and climbing up happens only after a failure. Presets exist for three model families, and replacement inside a family follows price and context window. The most instructive part: for a month I believed the reasoning level worked, and a check showed it never reached the models. I built observation where each probe is tied to its own row of the request log and ran every rung from low to max: now I can show which level actually reached the model. The principle is the same everywhere: measure first, call it working later.

Executor Swarm and Code Pipeline

A big task is cut into pieces and each piece goes through roles: docs, study, writing, checking. A swarm of cheap models works in parallel, and there is one judge: the tests. Two modes: a raid (different models compete and the first to pass counts) and a cascade (a cheap wave first, a stronger one on failure). For quality I built a base of 'rakes' from open sources of vulnerabilities and bugs: 38,789 cards mixed into executor prompts. The most valuable part of this work is the measurements. Paired comparisons on identical tasks tested the foreman checklist, repeated attempts and an independent test written from the spec: no clever tweak gave a gain above noise, and none was kept. The real jump in success from 43% to 80–88% came from one thing: the model ladder.

Model Gateway — Entry Point, Locks and Paid Dashboard

A local routing gateway puts subscription and API models behind a single address: every agent talks to it rather than to vendors. On top of it I added a keeper that re-syncs the model catalogue every three hours and refreshes the lists on all devices, and a launch with no windows at all. The best debugging story: the gateway kept locking the whole channel for an hour while part of the models were answering. The cause was a vendor hint: it returned 429 with Retry-After for one dead model and the gateway locked the entire connection because of it. I turned off locking on such hints; a test of three consecutive failures from the dead model showed the others answering 200. Next to it sits a paid gateway for outside users: card, instant bank transfer and stablecoin payments, a dashboard with 2FA and backup codes, API keys with limits, streamed answers and webhook signature checks.

Trading Research — and the Strategies I Dropped

An honest validation method: train and test on separate periods, CPCV cross-validation, a blind segment that neither the developer nor the optimizer has seen, weekend price gaps accounted for. A live runner is deployed to a production server: a spread-to-stop filter, a trade-side gate, a frozen thread pool before rollout, atomic state writes. The negative results are the most telling. Early break-even raised the share of winning trades from 28% to 45% but cut long trends and lost on the blind segment: rejected. A wide scan across 67 symbols with a meta-filter lost out of sample: rejected. A run over 51 assets showed where the strategy has its top shelf and the bottom of the ranking, and weak instruments stay out of the portfolio. The conclusion: a result without a blind check is not a result.

Defence and Daily Sparring Against Attacks

My AI analyst reads server state and reports problems, which means it can be fooled. So it has a sparring partner: a red team built on a cheap model writes attacks of two kinds every day, persuading it that a real threat is harmless and raising false panic on a routine event. A breach is closed the same day and re-tested with the same attack. Difficulty keeps rising: direct injections, obfuscation, multi-field, two-turn, rotated tricks on a hold-out set, then attacks on the triage layer itself. The defence loop itself: read access without write rights, quarantine of external data, confirmation before dangerous actions, an append-only journal. On the production server password login is turned off: the log held 724 password guesses, zero foreign logins, and now only keys work.

Three Machines and a Phone — My Own Infrastructure

The 8 GB head PC is where I think and write. Anything heavy goes to a compute node with one command: connection only through encrypted tunnels, no port exposed publicly. The production server runs the trading services. A rooted phone is part of the shared network: a link over an SSH tunnel that starts outside any app and survives reboots. Services self-heal, and I do not accept 'fixed' without a crash test, so the journal holds both the killed processes and the time they took to return. A recent find: a watchdog judged a collector's life by log age, but the log is written less often than the threshold, so a healthy process kept getting killed (1,410 restarts in eight days). The collector now writes a heartbeat every 15 seconds and the watchdog reads it; a crash test showed a killed process back up in about two minutes.
